Analysis

AI did not develop in a straight line. Three forces drove every major breakthrough: big data, GPU hardware, and algorithmic advances. The 2012 AlexNet moment was the convergence of all three. Before it, two AI winters demonstrated that ambition without infrastructure produces stagnation. Cloud platforms like AWS, Google Cloud, and Azure then made large-scale training accessible without requiring supercomputers.

Key players — OpenAI, Google DeepMind, NVIDIA, Anthropic, Meta, and Microsoft — each contributed a different layer of the stack. The EU AI Act in 2024 marks the first time regulation has caught up, signaling that responsible AI is now both an ethical expectation and a legal one.

Key Insight

"AI progress depends not only on ideas but on funding, computing power, and realistic expectations. The AI winters were not failures of imagination — they were failures of infrastructure readiness. Every modern breakthrough traces back to the moment hardware finally caught up with the vision."

My Reflection

Building this timeline shifted how I think about the technology I work with every day. As a QA engineer on mainframe systems, I was already working with infrastructure built in an era before modern AI. Mapping the full arc from the Turing Test to ChatGPT helped me see that the tools emerging now are not replacements for rigorous engineering: they are extensions of it. What surprised me most was the AI winters. Periods where the ideas were right but the hardware and funding were not ready. That pattern resonates with QA experience too. Good design means nothing without the infrastructure to support it. This timeline gave me a clearer mental model of where I am starting my AI/ML journey and what it takes to build things that last.

Project Overview

This artifact was adapted from a class assignment in Machine Learning Fundamentals and revised for a professional audience. It demonstrates a working understanding of how machine learning and deep learning differ — and, more importantly, when each is the right tool for a real business problem.

ML vs. DL at a Glance
Machine Learning
Structured · Interpretable
Algorithms that learn patterns from data — decision trees, random forests, gradient boosting, SVMs. They excel on tabular, structured inputs and stay explainable to auditors and stakeholders.
  • Trains on structured / tabular data
  • Feature engineering is explicit and auditable
  • Fast to train, cheap to run, easy to explain
Deep Learning
Raw · Representation-Learning
Multi-layer neural networks that learn their own features directly from raw inputs — images, audio, text. They shine where patterns are too complex for hand-crafted features, at the cost of data, compute, and interpretability.
  • Learns directly from raw pixels / signal
  • Handles high-dimensional, unstructured data
  • Needs large data + GPU compute
Two Case Studies
Machine Learning

Banking Fraud Detection

Transaction data is structured: amounts, merchants, geolocation, device, history. Models like gradient-boosted trees score millions of events in milliseconds and surface why a flag fired — critical for a regulated bank.

Why ML fits
Tabular data, tight latency, and audit-grade explainability make classic ML the pragmatic, compliant choice.
Why DL is overkill here
A deep network adds training cost and a black-box risk with little gain on clean tabular signals — and regulators want to know why.
Deep Learning

Autonomous-Driving Vision

Raw camera frames are unstructured pixels. Convolutional neural networks learn lane markings, pedestrians, and signs straight from the image — patterns no one could hand-code at scale.

Why DL fits
The task is perceptual and high-dimensional; representation learning is the only realistic path to the accuracy required.
Why simple ML struggles
Shallow models need manually engineered features and still miss the nuance of raw visual context — unsafe at driving stakes.

Objective

Albert Einstein is credited with saying that if you can't explain something simply, you don't understand it well enough. This artifact takes on that challenge: a jargon-free explanation of how neural networks learn, written for a reader with no ML background, produced through disciplined collaboration with generative AI. It demonstrates a competency distinct from Artifacts 1 and 2 — not researching AI history or analyzing model selection, but making AI understandable, the skill that decides whether teams actually adopt what engineers build.

The Artifact: A Team of Small Specialists

A neural network is not magic; it is a system of simple parts working together to recognize patterns and improve through feedback. If I had to explain a neural network one simple way, I would compare it to a team of small specialists working together to solve a problem. Each specialist looks at only one piece of the information, but together they help make a final decision. In machine learning, those specialists are called neurons, organized into layers that pass information forward.

Imagine a large company deciding whether to approve a loan. The application does not go to one genius who decides everything. It moves through teams: one clerk checks income, another checks credit history, another checks debt. Each passes a small opinion upward — "looks fine to me," "slightly risky" — and managers above them combine those opinions, give some more weight than others, and pass judgment further up. At the top, someone makes the final call: approve or deny. In a network, how strongly each opinion counts is set by numbers called weights — like how much a manager trusts each team member.

The clever part is training. When the company gets a decision wrong, feedback flows back down the chain — every team slightly adjusts how much it trusts each source going forward. In machine learning, that backward flow of error is called backpropagation. After millions of decisions and corrections, this company of simple workers becomes surprisingly effective, even though no single employee ever became especially smart. That is the whole secret: a neural network is not one big brain — it is a large team of tiny, specialized parts organized so well that, as a whole, it becomes brilliant.

Data Cleaning & Preprocessing
Missing & Outliers

Handle Messy Records

Real data is rarely clean. Missing values are dropped only when rare and random; otherwise I impute (mean/median) or flag them so the model knows they were absent. Outliers are investigated before deletion — a $9,999 transaction may be fraud, not a typo.

Class Imbalance

When 98% Is a Trap

If 98% of tickets are "resolved," a model that always predicts "resolved" scores 98% accuracy yet catches nothing. I handle imbalance with class weights, resampling (SMOTE), and metrics beyond accuracy — precision, recall, F1.

Model Selection & Evaluation
Choosing & Justifying
Fit the problem
Model choice follows the problem: structured, tabular, explainability-sensitive data → gradient boosting or logistic regression; raw images/audio → deep learning. In a bank, a less-accurate-but-explainable model is often the right call so risk teams can govern it.
False Positives vs Negatives
Cost drives the threshold
Accuracy lies on imbalanced data. A false positive is a false alarm; a false negative is a missed event. Which hurts more is domain-specific — so I set the decision threshold by business cost, not a default 0.5.
Privacy, Security & Bias
Privacy & Security

Protect the Training Data

Training data often carries PII — customer names, account context. I minimize columns, tokenize/pseudonymize, restrict access, and use synthetic data for development, keeping training data inside the regulated environment. That discipline comes straight from USAA mainframe work.

Bias Mitigation

Audit for Representation

Biased historical data yields a biased model. I audit training data for coverage and check outcomes across groups. If "success" labels come only from one business unit, the model learns that unit's pattern as "normal" and misreads others.

Post-Deployment Monitoring

A model degrades as data changes. My monitoring plan tracks input distributions, accuracy, and false-positive/false-negative rates over time; sets alert thresholds; schedules retraining; keeps a human review on high-stakes outputs; and logs overrides — where humans correct the model. Overrides are where hidden bias and drift surface first. In QA terms, it is the same discipline as monitoring a production system after release: the work is not done at deployment, it begins there.
